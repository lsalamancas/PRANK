\chapter{MATERIALS AND METHODS}\label{methods}
\graphicspath{{images/}}

This chapter describes the system architecture and development methodology of PRANK — a Parallel Robot for ANKle rehabilitation.
The project is structured in five sequential stages that span mechanical design, electronic integration, control implementation, mixed-reality interface development, and experimental validation.
This staged structure supports project scheduling and provides clear deliverables and entry/exit criteria at each phase.
Development follows established engineering best practices for mechatronic systems and rehabilitation robotics, with safety and software quality considerations aligned with relevant international standards where applicable.

\section{Stage 1 — Mechanical Design and Fabrication}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The PRANK robot is a three-degree-of-freedom (DoF) parallel structure designed to produce controlled rotational motion at the ankle joint in three anatomical planes: dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, inversion/eversion, and internal/external rotation.
The kinematic configuration follows an RRR (revolute--revolute--revolute) chain arrangement, in which three independent motor-driven arms converge on a common moving platform — the footplate — through spherical or universal joints.

The mechanical structure is constructed from lightweight aluminium profiles and 3D-printed structural joints, combining rigidity with modularity and ease of component replacement.
The footplate includes an adjustable strapping system and a contoured sole support to accommodate different foot sizes and ensure reliable mechanical coupling between the patient's foot and the robot.
Rotation centre alignment with the anatomical ankle joint centre is a primary design constraint, implemented through geometric calibration of the kinematic chain during the design phase.

The full 3D mechanical design is developed in \textbf{SolidWorks}, which is also used for structural simulation (FEA) to verify that the frame, joints, and footplate withstand the peak loads reported in the biomechanics literature (up to $\sim112$~Nm at the ankle during push-off equivalent loading).
Kinematic workspace coverage — at minimum $\pm25^{\circ}$ dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, $\pm15^{\circ}$ inversion/eversion, and $\pm10^{\circ}$ internal/external rotation (H1) — is verified through SolidWorks Motion analysis prior to fabrication.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Assembled and mechanically validated parallel robotic platform.

\section{Stage 2 — Electronic Integration and Sensing}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Each of the three kinematic chains is actuated by a \textbf{brushless DC motor} with an integrated incremental encoder, providing position feedback with sufficient resolution for ankle-level kinematics ($<0.1^{\circ}$ angular resolution at the footplate).
Motor drivers communicate with the embedded controller via a CAN bus, providing a noise-robust, real-time communication channel suitable for multi-axis coordinated motion.

Force and torque sensing at the robot--patient interface is performed by a \textbf{six-axis load cell} (also referred to as a 6-DoF force/torque sensor) mounted between the footplate and the last structural link.
Unlike a 3D force sensor — which measures only the three linear force components ($F_x, F_y, F_z$) — a six-axis load cell additionally measures the three torque components ($M_x, M_y, M_z$), providing the full interaction wrench required by the admittance controller to detect rotational intent at the ankle.
This distinction is critical: without the torque channels, the controller cannot distinguish whether the patient is attempting dorsiflexion from inversion, making a 6-axis sensor necessary despite its higher cost.
Analog signals from the load cell are conditioned and digitised by a dedicated ADC front-end before transmission to the controller.

The embedded processing platform is a dual-core \textbf{STM32} microcontroller.
The two cores are assigned distinct, real-time tasks to avoid scheduling conflicts and ensure deterministic execution:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core 1 — Sensing and data acquisition:} Reads encoder positions, load cell measurements, and any auxiliary sensors at a fixed sampling rate; performs signal filtering and packaging; and passes processed data to Core 2 via shared memory with a hardware semaphore.
    \item \textbf{Core 2 — Control and communications:} Executes the admittance control law, generates motor torque commands, manages safety monitors (torque limits, emergency stop), and handles serial communication with the Unity game engine on the host PC.
\end{itemize}

This dual-core partitioning ensures that sensing and control loops run at their required update rates independently, preventing communication overhead from degrading control performance.
Firmware is developed in \textbf{C/C++} using the STM32 HAL and FreeRTOS for task scheduling.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Fully wired and calibrated mechatronic platform with verified sensor readings and motor control.

\section{Stage 3 — Control System Implementation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The control architecture implements three rehabilitation modes — passive, assistive, and resistive — all built on the admittance control framework described in the theoretical framework (Equation~\ref{eq:admittance}).

In \textbf{passive mode}, the robot executes a predefined reference trajectory (sinusoidal or custom) with high virtual stiffness $K_d$, guiding the ankle through the target ROM regardless of patient effort.
In \textbf{assistive mode}, virtual stiffness is reduced and the admittance law allows the patient's voluntary interaction forces to modify the prescribed trajectory, encouraging active participation.
In \textbf{resistive mode}, a velocity-proportional virtual damping $B_d$ opposes the patient's motion, providing progressive mechanical resistance for strength training.

The admittance control law and mode-switching logic are implemented directly in \textbf{C/C++} on the STM32 platform.
Controller gains and admittance parameters are tuned experimentally through a systematic parameter sweep on the physical prototype, and performance is assessed against the criteria in H2: settling time $<500$~ms and force tracking error $<15\%$ of commanded torque.
Offline data analysis and result plotting are performed in MATLAB.

Safety mechanisms implemented in firmware include: per-axis torque saturation, workspace boundary enforcement, watchdog timer, and a hardware emergency-stop circuit.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Implemented, tuned, and safety-validated admittance control system operating in all three rehabilitation modes.

\section{Stage 4 — Mixed Reality Interface Development}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The patient-facing interface is a \textbf{mixed reality (MR) serious game} developed in \textbf{Unity} with \textbf{C\#}.
The game world is fully virtual — the patient is not shown their foot or the robot — and the ankle motion is mapped to the control of an in-game character or object, creating an intuitive and engaging rehabilitation experience.

The system supports two display modalities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virtual reality (VR) mode:} full immersive display via a head-mounted display (HMD), providing a completely virtual environment.
    \item \textbf{Mixed reality (MR) mode:} virtual game elements are overlaid on the physical environment using a passthrough HMD, allowing the patient to remain aware of their surroundings while playing.
\end{itemize}

Game design follows an \textbf{arcade paradigm}: fast-paced, visually clear games inspired by classical titles where the ankle controls the game action directly.
Three game archetypes are planned, each targeting a different movement dimensionality:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1D games} (e.g., Flappy Bird--style): dorsiflexion/plantarflexion only controls altitude or height of an object. Used in early rehabilitation stages or when only one axis has sufficient motor control.
    \item \textbf{2D games} (e.g., car driving, side-scrolling): two ankle axes (e.g., plantarflexion/dorsiflexion + inversion/eversion) control steering and speed simultaneously, requiring coordinated biaxial movement.
    \item \textbf{3D games} (e.g., air hockey, ball rolling): all three ankle axes are mapped to full 3D object control, requiring the most complex and complete ankle motor activation.
\end{itemize}

Progression between 1D, 2D, and 3D modes is decided by the therapist based on the patient's ROM and force metrics logged across sessions.
The game communicates with the STM32 Core 2 over a serial USB link, receiving real-time ankle joint angles at a rate sufficient for smooth visual feedback ($\geq60$~Hz).
To reinforce motor learning, the interface implements \textbf{multimodal biofeedback} across three sensory channels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual alerts:} on-screen indicators (colour changes, flashes, score events) signal when the patient reaches a ROM target, exceeds a force threshold, or completes a game objective.
    \item \textbf{Spatial audio:} directional sound cues tied to game events — success tones, collision effects, rhythm cues — reinforce correct movement patterns and provide timing information independent of visual attention.
    \item \textbf{Vibrotactile feedback:} haptic actuator on the footplate or HMD provides a brief vibration pulse upon target achievement, offering a proprioceptive confirmation channel that remains effective even when the patient's visual focus is occupied by the game.
\end{itemize}
A therapist dashboard allows session configuration, mode selection, biofeedback intensity adjustment, and review of performance metrics (ROM achieved, task completion rate, applied force profile).

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Functional MR serious game with three arcade archetypes (1D/2D/3D), integrated with the robot hardware, and therapist dashboard operational.

\section{Stage 5 — System Integration and Experimental Validation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The final stage integrates all subsystems and conducts the experimental protocol described in the project hypotheses.

\textbf{H1 — Workspace validation:} The robot is commanded to its mechanical limits in each axis and the achieved angles are measured with the on-board encoders and confirmed visually. The target values ($\pm25^{\circ}$, $\pm15^{\circ}$, $\pm10^{\circ}$) must be reached without binding or motor saturation.

\textbf{H2 — Controller validation:} A series of step-reference and sinusoidal tracking experiments are performed with a mechanical ankle phantom and subsequently with healthy subjects.
Force tracking error and settling time are computed from the logged sensor data and compared against the H2 thresholds.

\textbf{H3 — ROM validation:} A pilot study with healthy adult subjects is conducted simultaneously using the robot's encoder-based ROM estimation and a reference optoelectronic motion capture system.
Both systems record ankle joint angles across passive ROM trials.
Pearson correlation coefficient and RMSE between the two measurement systems are computed and compared against the H3 thresholds ($r > 0.95$, RMSE $< 5^{\circ}$).

All experimental data are logged to file by Core 2 and the Unity dashboard for offline analysis in MATLAB.
Results are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across subjects and trials.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Experimental dataset and statistical analysis report validating H1 and H3 (H2 is validated at the end of Stage 3).
