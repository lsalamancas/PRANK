\chapter{MATERIALS AND METHODS}\label{methods}
\graphicspath{{images/}}

This chapter describes the system architecture and development methodology of PRANK — a Parallel Robot for ANKle rehabilitation.
The project is structured in four sequential stages spanning mechanical design, electronic architecture specification, embedded control implementation, and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulation validation.
In the HIL configuration, the microcontroller development board runs the production admittance control firmware and interacts in real time with a ROS~2/Gazebo simulation that provides virtual sensor readings (encoders, force/torque) and actuates the simulated robot based on the firmware commands — replacing the physical motors and sensors that will be integrated in Phase~2.
This staged structure supports project scheduling and provides clear deliverables and entry/exit criteria at each phase.

Figure~\ref{fig:PRANK_architecture} presents the overall system architecture, showing the integration between the mechanical platform, embedded hardware, and the computational simulation layer.

\section{Stage 1 — Mechanical Design and CAD Modelling}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The PRANK robot is a three-degree-of-freedom (DoF) parallel structure designed to produce controlled rotational motion at the ankle joint in three anatomical planes: dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, inversion/eversion, and internal/external rotation.
The kinematic configuration follows an RRR (revolute--revolute--revolute) chain arrangement, in which three independent motor-driven arms converge on a common moving platform — the footplate — through spherical or universal joints.

The mechanical structure is constructed from lightweight aluminium profiles and 3D-printed structural joints, combining rigidity with modularity and ease of component replacement.
The footplate includes an adjustable strapping system and a contoured sole support to accommodate different foot sizes and ensure reliable mechanical coupling between the patient's foot and the robot.
Rotation centre alignment with the anatomical ankle joint centre is a primary design constraint, implemented through geometric calibration of the kinematic chain during the design phase.

The full 3D mechanical design is developed in \textbf{SolidWorks}, which is also used for structural simulation (FEA) to verify that the frame, joints, and footplate withstand the peak loads reported in the biomechanics literature (up to $\sim112$~Nm at the ankle during push-off equivalent loading).
Kinematic workspace coverage — at minimum $\pm25^{\circ}$ dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, $\pm15^{\circ}$ inversion/eversion, and $\pm10^{\circ}$ internal/external rotation (H1) — is verified through SolidWorks Motion analysis.
The resulting CAD model is exported in URDF/SDF format for import into the ROS~2/Gazebo simulation environment.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Complete SolidWorks CAD model with FEA report, workspace verification results, and URDF/SDF simulation export.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 2 — Electronic Architecture Design}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Each of the three kinematic chains is actuated by a \textbf{brushless DC motor} with an integrated incremental encoder, providing position feedback with sufficient resolution for ankle-level kinematics ($<0.1^{\circ}$ angular resolution at the footplate).
Motor drivers communicate with the embedded controller via a CAN bus, providing a noise-robust, real-time communication channel suitable for multi-axis coordinated motion.

Force and torque sensing at the robot--patient interface is performed by a \textbf{six-axis load cell} mounted between the footplate and the last structural link, providing the full interaction wrench ($F_x, F_y, F_z, M_x, M_y, M_z$) required by the admittance controller to detect rotational intent at the ankle.
Analog signals from the load cell are conditioned and digitised by a dedicated ADC front-end before transmission to the controller.

The embedded processing platform is a dual-core \textbf{microcontroller}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core 1 — Sensing and data acquisition:} Reads encoder positions, load cell measurements, and auxiliary sensors at a fixed sampling rate; performs signal filtering and packaging; passes processed data to Core~2 via shared memory with hardware semaphore.
    \item \textbf{Core 2 — Control and communications:} Executes the admittance control law, generates motor torque commands, manages safety monitors, and handles serial communication with the ROS~2 simulation host.
\end{itemize}

In this phase, the electronic design is fully specified — including schematic, PCB layout, and bill of materials — but \textbf{physical board fabrication and assembly are deferred to Phase~2}.
A commercially available microcontroller development board is used in Stage~4 for HIL validation.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Complete electronic design package (schematic, PCB layout, BOM) and microcontroller firmware architecture ready for HIL deployment.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Stage 3 — Embedded Control Implementation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The admittance control law and mode-switching logic are implemented in \textbf{C/C++} directly on the microcontroller platform under \textbf{FreeRTOS}.
The control architecture implements three rehabilitation modes — passive, assistive, and resistive — all built on the admittance framework described in the theoretical framework (Equation~\ref{eq:admittance}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Passive mode:} high virtual stiffness $K_d$ guides the ankle through a predefined reference trajectory regardless of patient effort.
    \item \textbf{Assistive mode:} reduced stiffness allows the patient's voluntary interaction forces to modify the prescribed trajectory, encouraging active participation.
    \item \textbf{Resistive mode:} velocity-proportional virtual damping $B_d$ opposes patient motion, providing progressive mechanical resistance for strength training.
\end{itemize}

Safety mechanisms implemented in firmware include: per-axis torque saturation, workspace boundary enforcement, watchdog timer, and a software emergency-stop interface.

In the HIL setup, the microcontroller firmware receives simulated sensor data from the ROS~2/Gazebo plant (joint angles from virtual encoders and interaction forces from the simulated load cell) over a USB serial link, computes the admittance control output, and returns motor commands that are applied back to the Gazebo simulation.
From the firmware's perspective, the simulation is indistinguishable from real hardware, exercising the full embedded control loop including interrupt timing, FreeRTOS task scheduling, and CAN-bus driver logic.

Figure~\ref{fig:sim_environment} illustrates the HIL simulation architecture.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} Embedded admittance control firmware running on microcontroller development board, integrated with ROS~2/Gazebo HIL loop.

\section{Stage 4 — HIL Validation and Performance Evaluation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The final stage validates the full HIL loop and evaluates the admittance control system against the project hypotheses.

\textbf{H1 — Workspace verification:} The kinematic model is commanded to its boundary poses in each axis within the SolidWorks Motion and Gazebo simulation environments, and achieved angles are compared against the H1 thresholds ($\pm25^{\circ}$, $\pm15^{\circ}$, $\pm10^{\circ}$).

\textbf{H2 — Controller performance:} Step-reference and sinusoidal tracking experiments are executed through the HIL loop with the microcontroller running the production firmware.
Force tracking error and settling time are computed from ROS~2 bag data and compared against the H2 thresholds (settling time $<500$~ms, error $<15\%$).

\textbf{H3 — HIL fidelity:} Joint trajectories produced by the HIL configuration are compared to those from a pure-software reference simulation under identical input sequences.
Root mean square deviation must remain below $2^{\circ}$ and USB serial round-trip latency below 10~ms, confirming the embedded implementation is ready for physical hardware integration in Phase~2.

All data are logged via ROS~2 bag files and processed offline in MATLAB/Python.
Results are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across trials.

\textbf{Stage deliverable:} HIL validation dataset and statistical analysis report confirming H1, H2, and H3.
